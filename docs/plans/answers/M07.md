# M07 answers — Workflows & sagas

1. **No 2PC.** It needs every participant to support XA/2PC (most brokers, cloud services and HTTP APIs don't), holds locks across the network for the whole protocol (latency, contention), the coordinator is a single point of failure (blocked participants if it dies mid-protocol), and availability drops as participants are added. Business processes that wait minutes/hours for humans can't hold locks anyway.
2. **Orchestration vs choreography.** Orchestration fits a multi-step process with timeouts, branching and compensation that the business wants visible and changeable as one thing (dispatch: propose → accept/timeout → escalate). Choreography fits simple, loosely coupled reactions where no one needs to coordinate (`ShipmentDelivered` → Billing invoices, Notifications emails). Choreography for long processes becomes hard to see and debug.
3. **Compensation ≠ rollback.** Rollback erases changes as if they never happened (inside one DB transaction). Compensation is a *new* forward action that semantically undoes the business effect, which is visible and recorded. Example: a driver was already notified and the vehicle reserved; on cancellation you send "assignment cancelled" and release the reservation. You can't un-send the notification.
4. **Idempotent transitions.** Each handler checks the current state and the message (ignore `DriverAccepted` if already accepted or the saga is completed), records processed message ids, and uses optimistic concurrency on saga state. Needed because messages are delivered at least once and can arrive late or in parallel; without it a duplicate could re-trigger side effects (double reservations or notifications).
5. **Timeouts.** When entering the waiting state, schedule a delayed/timeout message (e.g. in 15 min) correlated to the saga. When it arrives, act only if the saga is still waiting. If the real reply arrives after the timeout, the saga is already in "escalated"; define the rule explicitly (ignore and tell the driver it expired, or accept if no reassignment happened yet). Concurrency control makes "first wins" deterministic.
6. **DbContext in BackgroundService.** A `BackgroundService` is a singleton and `DbContext` is scoped: a captive dependency, so one context lives forever (memory growth, stale tracking, not thread-safe), and `ValidateScopes` fails at startup. Inject `IServiceScopeFactory` and create a scope (or use `IDbContextFactory`) per unit of work/iteration.
7. **Single-runner job.** A distributed lock: Postgres `pg_try_advisory_lock(key)` (or a lease row with expiry), Redis lock (with care), or a scheduler with clustering (Quartz with a DB job store, Hangfire, Wolverine scheduled/leader election). Plus make the job idempotent in case two runs ever overlap.
8. **Saga operations tooling.** Visibility of saga instances and their states (a query/admin view), detection of stuck sagas (no transition for X time) with alerts, the ability to manually retry/advance/cancel a saga, DLQ replay for its messages, tracing/correlation ids across steps, and metrics (duration, timeouts, compensations).
9. **Saga overkill.** When the work fits in one local transaction (same module/DB), or for 2–3 quick synchronous steps with no waiting and no meaningful compensation, or when simple choreography (one event, one reaction) suffices. Don't distribute a problem you can keep local.
10. **Travel booking.** An orchestrated saga: reserve hotel → flight → car, each a *hold* with expiry (a 10-min timeout). On any failure, cancel the holds already made (compensations, idempotent). On success, confirm all (confirmation can also fail, so retry and possibly refund). Order the steps by "most likely to fail / hardest to compensate first". Persist state, use timeouts for holds, idempotency keys per provider call, and give the user a clear status page.
